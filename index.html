2

Getting your thoughts across

The most important part. Claude can build almost anything, but it cannot guess what you meant. What comes back depends mostly on how well you said what you wanted.

Just say it, with Wispr Flow

Wispr Flow (free) turns what you say into clean text in any app. Hold a key and talk it through. You get your whole thought process across without worrying about every word, and Claude reads the intent. This is how Jacob works.

Say who and why

"A freshman with no resume opens Job Match and has no idea where to start" gets a far better result than "make Job Match better". Start with the student and the moment they are in.

Show it

Claude mostly reads code. It does not see the page the way you do, so show it: a screenshot of what looks wrong, a link to something you like, a component you want. "Like this, but for our Events page" beats a paragraph.

Say what you are reacting to

Not just "I don't like it". Say why: "three things fight for attention, so I don't know where to look first". That is what Claude can fix.

Say how sure you are

"Make this cleaner" is direction, and Claude comes back with options. "Specifically, make the button red" is exact, and it does just that. "Just go" means build it without checking in first.

One thing at a time

One change per ask. Five changes in one message come back as five half-done changes and no way to tell which one broke something.

3

The work

Testing comes first, because it trains the core skill: noticing when something is wrong for a student and saying exactly what. Jacob will walk you through Base, where all of this lives.

TestingWhenever a test run arrives

When the team ships something, a test run shows up in Base with your name on it. Go through it one feature at a time and answer Works, Something's off, or Can't test this, then give it a 1 to 10. Be a specific student, not yourself: a freshman with no resume, or someone on a phone between classes. Look at what a page shows before you have saved anything, try to break it, and talk out loud as you go. Anything you notice outside a run goes through the Feedback button in CampusAI. Your fresh eyes are the most useful thing you have, and they fade in about a week.

GradingEvery Thursday

Twice a week a routine finds new components and puts each on a card at elevations.vercel.app. Score each one 1 to 10, with one sentence on why and where in CampusAI it would go. Jacob's score stays hidden until you give yours.

BuildingYour own time

Find something you like, pick one place in CampusAI it would make better, and say it in one sentence: "The Save button on Job Match should fill like this." Open Claude in your copy, paste the link and the sentence, and say add a component.

4

Iterating

Nothing good comes out right the first time. The first version is a draft, and good work usually takes several rounds. Every piece of work goes through this loop.

1Say it

Who it is for, what is wrong for them, and what you want instead. One thing.

2See options first

Ask Claude to find two or three ways others already solved it. Borrow before you build.

3Pick one

Choose the option, or the best parts of two. That call is yours, not Claude's.

4Claude builds it

In your copy of CampusAI, with the real colours, fonts and components.

5Look and evaluate

Say what you expect, then press it, on a phone width too. Run the checks in the next section.

6Go again, then ship

Say exactly what is off and repeat. When it is right, put it on your link and send Jacob the link and your one sentence.

5

Evaluating the output

Always be looking, evaluating, iterating. Claude sounds just as sure when it is wrong as when it is right, so look at what it built and decide whether it is actually right. Each check is here because skipping it already cost us something.

The habit that makes it easy: say what you expect before you look. "When I press Add, the event should show up in Your week." Then press it and compare. Without an expectation, whatever appears looks fine.

1"Done" is a claim

Open the link and press the thing yourself. Never pass along "it works" that you did not see.

2Look for what disappeared

A change can quietly remove something. One of ours deleted an onboarding question meant to stay.

3Saved is not used

A button said Saved while the job feed never moved, because nothing read what it saved.

4Your link may only work for you

It opens because you are logged in. Open it in a private window before sending it.

5Perfect data hides problems

Try a long name, an empty account, a phone width, the back button, pressing twice.

6Words must match the screen

We once shipped "Roles matching zzzzz" over finance roles. Read every label as the student.

7Check any "it says so"

We called a waitlist live because a code comment said so. It had been deleted months earlier.

8Then go again

Finding something off is the job, not a failure. Say exactly what is wrong, and look again.

What to ask Claude

"What did you change, what did you remove, and what did you not check?" "Show me it at phone width, and in dark mode." "What exactly did you click to confirm it works?" "If this was wrong, how would we know?"

How to report it

Say which checks you ran and which you did not. "I checked it on a phone and in a private window, I did not try an empty account" is a good report. "Done" is not.

6

How the connections work

A connection (people call them MCPs) is a plug that lets Claude use another tool directly. Without one, Claude only knows what it remembers. With one, it can look something up or do something for you, live.

You just ask

You never call a connection yourself. Say what you want in plain words, like "search the component libraries for a button that fills while you hold it" or "put this on my Vercel", and Claude picks the right one.

What yours can do

Search eight component libraries and pull the real code. Look at real websites and Motion's examples. Read up-to-date docs and explain any GitHub repo. Open what it built and check it at phone width. Deploy to Vercel, use Supabase, and drive Raylight for short product videos.

Seeing and signing in

Type /mcp in a chat to see every connection and whether it works. Vercel, Supabase and Raylight ask you to sign in in your browser the first time Claude uses them.

8

Rules, and what to say first

Four rules that never move, and a few things to try once you are set up.

Push only to your own copy

Your copy of the demo is yours. Nothing else.

"I'm testing the calendar. Help me go through it like a freshman would."
Deploy only to your own Vercel

Never anyone else's account.

"Here's my voice memo. Turn it into feedback I can send."
No real student information

Not in a prompt, a file or a build. Make up a sample student.

"Find three ways other apps do a filter bar, then build the best one in our style."
Jacob sees it first

Nothing goes into the real product, gets posted, or reaches students before he does.

"Is this good? Check it against our hard lines."
